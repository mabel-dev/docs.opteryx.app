# Our Regex Matcher Wasn't Slow at Matching. It Was Slow at Waiting.

## TL;DR

Our deterministic finite automaton (DFA) matcher for `RLIKE` ran at roughly 4-5 CPU cycles per byte, and it did so whatever the pattern. That flat line was the clue: the transition tables fit in L1, so table size wasn't the problem. The per-byte dependency chain was. We attacked it three ways: a required-literal prefilter, skipping bytes in states that have almost nowhere to go, and walking eight rows in lockstep. On 100 million ClickBench rows, `RLIKE` queries now take 0.26-0.74x of their old time on an Apple M5 Pro and 0.37-0.87x on an x86 i5. These are development-build numbers on top of 0.9.175, not a released version, and the 8-lane interleave is measured on the Mac only. Some patterns didn't move at all, and the post says which.

## The Problem

In [When we Stopped Using Regex for REGEXP_REPLACE](/blog/2026-04-24-regex-replace) we replaced a backtracking regex engine with a DFA, because most patterns don't need backtracking. It worked. It also left us with a matcher we'd only measured by whether the query got faster.

So before touching it again, we measured the matcher on its own: a harness that includes the kernel header directly, a single templated loop for every engine variant so the wrappers are identical, 2 million non-empty rows each of `URL`, `Referer`, `Title` and `SearchPhrase` from ClickBench, single thread, minimum of seven runs.

The headline was uncomfortable:

| Column | Row length | v1 DFA, unanchored |
|---|---|---|
| URL | ~90 B | 89-115 ns/row |
| Title | ~134 B | 172 ns/row |

That's about 1.1-1.3 ns per byte, or 4-5 cycles per byte. On the SQL side, at 100 million rows, the matcher was about 85% of the wall time of a `DFA RLIKE` query. Everything else (the scan, the filter, the output) was in the noise by comparison.

## The Obvious Theory Was Wrong

The obvious suspect for a slow table-driven automaton is the table. Big state counts, big tables, cache misses.

We checked. Real patterns compile to 4-25 states. The tables are at most 13 KB. They fit in L1 with room to spare. And the speed was *flat* across those state counts: a 4-state pattern was as slow per byte as a 25-state one.

If shrinking the table can't help, the table isn't the lever. What the numbers described instead was a serial chain, per byte:

1. load the next transition, which depends on the current state
2. compare against the dead-state marker
3. test the accept bitmap

Each byte's load can't start until the previous byte's load has finished. The CPU isn't busy, it's waiting. That's latency-bound, not throughput-bound, which is why the cost per byte looked like a constant.

(Same shape of lesson as the REGEXP_REPLACE post: the engine wasn't the bottleneck. This time the *table* wasn't.)

## Three Ways to Wait Less

### 1. Don't start the walk: a required-literal prefilter

Most patterns contain a literal that any match *must* contain. `google\.(com|ru)` can only match a row containing `google.`. Finding that with a vectorised substring search is much faster than walking an automaton over every byte, and most rows don't contain it at all.

So the compiled program carries the required literal, and the matcher searches for it first. Rows that can't match never enter the DFA.

Conditions we were strict about: the prefilter is only emitted when the start state isn't anchored and the literal is at least two bytes long. A one-byte "literal" is a worse filter than the walk it was supposed to avoid.

### 2. Skip bytes: acceleration for states that mostly stay put

Many DFA states have one or two bytes that make them do anything, and every other byte loops back to the same state. A state that is waiting for `.` in a long URL is the typical case.

For states with at most three "escape" bytes, the walk hands over to `memchr`, or a NEON or SSE2 scan, and jumps straight to the next escape byte. The per-byte chain is replaced by a vector scan.

To make the plain walk cheaper too, the transition entries carry flag bits (early accept, accelerated, dead), so the hot loop does a single compare per byte, and the program is decoded once per call.

### 3. Walk eight rows at once

Neither of those helps when a pattern has no required literal and no accelerable states: `[0-9]{5}` and friends. There, the chain is the problem, so we broke it.

`match_rows<L>` walks L rows in lockstep. Eight independent chains give the CPU eight loads in flight instead of one. The run length per step is the shortest live lane's remaining bytes, so there is no per-byte end-of-row test, and a single OR-and-test per step detects any lane hitting a special transition. Lanes that finish refill with the next row.

On ARM, L=8 was the best of {4, 6, 8, 12, 16}.

And here is the loss we'd rather you saw: forcing the interleave on patterns that already had acceleration or an anchored start was **1.1-2.0x slower**. The interleaved walk gives up the early exits that make those patterns cheap. So it's gated: interleaving applies only when the pattern isn't anchored at the start and has no accelerated state. That's derived when the program is decoded, with no change to the blob format.

## Results

All numbers are minimum or interleaved-alternating runs; every arm is bit-identical to v1 on the full row set, plus a 65-pattern x 5-column differential test (and a 315-case differential for the interleave, including under Rosetta for the SSE2 path).

**Kernel, new/old time:**

| Pattern class | Apple M5 Pro | Intel i5-8500 (x86, gcc 12.2, `-march=haswell`) |
|---|---|---|
| Has a required literal | 0.07-0.09 | 0.115-0.128 |
| Accelerated | 0.17-0.37 | 0.21-0.33 |
| Neither (encoding + decode-once only) | 0.63-0.72 | 0.60-0.73 |
| Neither, with 8-lane interleave | 0.31-0.44 | not measured |

**Whole queries, 100 million rows, interleaved A/B:**

| Query | Before | After | Ratio (Mac / x86) |
|---|---|---|---|
| `google\.(com\|ru)` | 483 ms (Mac), 3010 ms (x86) | 124 ms, 1117 ms | 0.26 / 0.371 |
| `\.(html\|php)$` | 434 ms | 169 ms | 0.39 / 0.454 |
| `[0-9]{5}` | 286 ms | 209 ms | 0.73 / 0.817 |
| four-way alternation | 239 ms | 174 ms | 0.73 / 0.868 |
| control (`URL <> ''`) | | | 1.00 / 1.00 |

The controls matter: they didn't move.

## What Didn't Work

- **Patterns with more than three escape bytes per state** (a four-way alternation, say) don't get acceleration. The cap is three.
- **`[0-9]{5}`-style classes** sit near the floor. A digit class has ten escape bytes, so there is nothing to skip.
- **On x86, the prefilter alone is no better than acceleration alone** (36 vs 34 ns). Our literal search has a NEON path but no AVX2 path; on x86 it's `memchr` plus `memcmp`. That's an open gap.
- **The DFA with a prefilter (7.8 ns/row) now beats the op-program (9.4 ns/row)** that production prefers for `%google%`. The planner's preference was set when the DFA was slower, and it now points the wrong way.
- **The i5 has the JCC erratum**, which makes layout noise of about 3-5% unavoidable. Every effect above is far outside that.

## Engineering Lessons

1. **A flat line is a finding.** Speed that didn't change with state count told us more than any profile: the lever wasn't the one we were pulling on.
2. **Latency-bound and throughput-bound are different problems.** A bigger cache, a smaller table, a faster ALU - none of those help a chain where every step waits on the last one. Independent work in flight does.
3. **Gate your specialisations.** The interleave is a 2-3x win on some patterns and a 2x loss on others. The gate is what makes it a feature rather than a regression.
4. **Measure on both architectures.** The ARM and x86 answers agree on direction and disagree on size, and one of them (the literal search) would have gone unnoticed on a single machine.

## What This Leaves Out

- The numbers are from a development build on top of 0.9.175, measured 2026-10-26 on an Apple M5 Pro (macOS 26.6) and an Intel i5-8500 (gcc 12.2, `-march=haswell`). The Mac was also running background load during the baseline, which is why every comparison is interleaved A/B rather than run-then-run.
- The 8-lane interleave has not been measured on x86 yet. Eight lanes may spill on x86's 16 general-purpose registers, so we won't assume the ARM result carries over.
- The benchmark harness is not published yet, so this post can't be reproduced from the repository today.

## Final Thought

The lesson from the last post was "recognise when you don't need a regex engine". This one is the next step down: even once you have an automaton, the work isn't the automaton. It's the wait between bytes.
